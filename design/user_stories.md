# User Stories

Each story is one short paragraph that answers four questions in order:

1. Who is the user.
2. What do they want to do with the tool.
3. What needs and desires do they want for the tool.
4. What is their skill level.

## Abhishek

1. Abhishek compares a new robot control model against a baseline in simulation. He wants a tool to read his automated logs and act as a strict referee against lucky streaks. He needs a lightweight checker to prevent false success claims without the overhead of heavy study-planning frameworks. Abhishek is an expert in Python but relies on established statistical methods.

2. Sarah tests a new manipulation policy on a physical robot. She wants a CLI tool to log each try and tell her exactly when to stop, minimizing manual scene resets. She needs a lightweight referee that translates raw stats into simple "stop" or "keep going" signals. Sarah knows Python but isn't a statistician.

3. David evaluates robot programs using public RoboArena datasets. He wants to automatically feed bulk trial records into a script to find exactly when a policy proved superior. He needs a tool that handles data imports and generates clean, publish-ready tables with error bars. David is highly proficient in Python scripting.

4. Dr. Elena directs a robotics lab and wants to standardize how students report policy improvements. She needs the tool to generate an easy-to-read final report showing confidence intervals so she can instantly verify claims and prevent cherry-picked results. Dr. Elena understands the statistics conceptually but does not write Python code.

5. Marcus maintains an automated evaluation pipeline for a shared robotics codebase. He wants a tool to automatically block GitHub pull requests if a new model isn't statistically better than the baseline. He needs it to run headless in CI/CD, ingest logs, and return strict system exit codes. Marcus is an expert in Python and MLOps but treats the statistics as a black box.

6. Jin competes in a public robotics challenge with a limited daily submission quota. He wants to compare local simulation runs to see if his latest policy tweak is genuinely better before submitting it to the official server. He needs a strict statistical referee that imports CSVs to prevent confirmation bias. Jin is advanced in Python and machine learning.

7. Chloe tests a navigation policy using two different physical cameras on a real-world obstacle course. She wants to stop manually walking the robot through the course as soon as one camera proves definitively better. She needs a responsive CLI that outputs a highly visible "STOP" signal while she manages the physical hardware. Chloe is an expert in ROS and Python but relies entirely on the tool for the math.

## Hao

1. Majilong is a second-year PhD student in a robot learning lab who tests manipulation policies on the lab's real robot arm. Every trial takes about a minute and a scene reset by hand, so she can afford only 20–40 trials per policy. She wants to log each trial right after she runs it, find out whether she already has enough evidence to stop, and be sure that none of the wins she reports is a lucky streak. She needs a clear answer after each trial (keep going, stop: new policy is better, or stop: no real difference) from a one-line command or a simple dashboard, clean records of every trial, and a final report with a results table, confidence intervals and plots ready to paste into her paper. Majilong writes Python every day and knows what a success rate and a p-value are, but has never studied sequential testing and does not want to become a statistician.

2. Leo is a machine learning engineer on a robotics team who evaluates versions of a vision-language-action policy on a simulation benchmark with dozens of tasks. A full evaluation sweep takes most of a day on the team's shared GPUs. He wants to call the tool from his existing evaluation script after every rollout, stop evaluating a policy–task pair as soon as the result is clear, and get one summary of which versions are better, worse, or no different. He needs a Python API that takes one rollout result at a time, correct handling of many comparisons at once, a study configuration file he can commit to the repository, and CSV and HTML outputs for the team's dashboards and weekly meetings. Leo is a strong Python developer who knows t-tests and confidence intervals, but not anytime-valid methods or multiple-comparison corrections.

3. Priya is a postdoc who writes related-work sections and reviews submissions for a robotics conference. She often reads claims such as "75% success, compared with 60% for the baseline" based on 20 trials. She wants to check whether reported improvements are real without running new experiments: enter counts such as "19/25" to see their confidence intervals, replay existing trial records in their original order, and compare a paper's claim with independent evaluation records. She needs an import command that reads a CSV or a public dataset and reports exactly which rows are invalid, a clear verdict for each claim (supported, not supported, or not enough data), a note when a paper does not report the number of trials, and a table and plot she can include in a review. Priya is comfortable with Python notebooks and has a solid statistics background, but does not want to write her own analysis code for every paper she reads.

## Kevin

1. Alex is a third-year PhD student in a manipulation lab, preparing a CoRL submission. The lab shares one Franka arm, so Alex can book only two afternoons a week. Alex wants to log each trial during a session and know, after every trial, which comparisons between the new policy and the baselines already have a conclusion, so that the remaining robot time goes only to comparisons that are still open. Alex needs the rules fixed before the first trial, a guarantee that covers all comparisons together and stays valid however often the results are checked, and a summary that goes into the paper without retyping. Alex writes Python every day and understands confidence intervals and p-values, but has not studied sequential tests or multiple-comparison corrections.

2. Sam is an undergraduate research assistant who runs trials for a PhD student: Sam places the objects, starts the policy, judges success or failure, and resets the scene. Sam wants to record each result within a few seconds of judging the trial, and to fix a wrong entry without calling anyone. Sam needs very little typing, a mistyped policy or task name caught on the spot, and recorded trials that survive a crash. Sam would also like to note how far the robot got and why it failed in the same step as the result. Sam is a second-year mechanical engineering student who can follow a checklist and type a short command, but does not write Python and knows no statistics.

3. Jordan is a research engineer who maintains a lab's LIBERO evaluation script. Every night the script runs a new checkpoint for thousands of simulated rollouts and writes the results to a CSV file. Jordan wants to know, for each new checkpoint, which tasks improved, which got worse, and which have too few rollouts to tell, without rewriting the script or rerunning anything. Jordan needs the tool to read the existing results file as it is, and would like to send each rollout from inside the script while a run is still going. Jordan is an expert Python programmer, but rarely uses confidence intervals and has never needed a multiple-comparison correction.

4. Riley is a postdoc writing a survey of vision-language-action policies for manipulation. Riley has no robot and works from the result tables in about ten papers and the public RoboArena records. Riley wants to check which published claims hold up, for example whether "ours 19/25 vs. baseline 14/20" is a real difference and whether paper claims agree with RoboArena head-to-head results. Riley needs intervals and a test for counts copied from papers, RoboArena records that load without hand conversion, comparisons on RoboArena's progress score, and one policy under different names treated as one. Riley would like to enter a whole survey table at once. Riley knows standard statistics well and works in Python notebooks with pandas, but does not want to learn a new data format.

5. Morgan is a professor who leads a robot learning lab and advises eight students. Morgan wants to read a student's report and decide within a minute whether the result is ready to submit, needs more trials, or should be dropped. Morgan needs every comparison's conclusion, trial count and certainty in one view, plus the rules the study used, so that a result tuned after the fact stands out. Morgan would also like to see why each policy failed, to steer the next round of work. Morgan is an expert in robot learning and understands confidence intervals, but never runs the tool and only reads an HTML report on a laptop or a projected slide.

6. Casey is a software developer who builds robots at home in the evenings and has trained two checkpoints of one policy on a low-cost SO-101 arm. Casey wants to find out whether the newer checkpoint is really better than the older one, and wants the tool to say when the evidence is enough. Casey needs to start a comparison without choosing any statistical settings, to be told how to set up trials so the comparison is valid, and to get the conclusion in plain words with a one-line reason. Casey would like error messages that say what to fix. Casey writes Python at work and can follow a tutorial, but knows no statistics and reads documentation only when stuck.

7. Quinn is a research engineer who maintains a public leaderboard for a simulated manipulation benchmark, where each submitted policy runs on 10 tasks with 50 episodes per task. Quinn wants the leaderboard to show which policies are actually different, instead of ranking them by gaps smaller than the noise. Quinn needs to compare many policies at once, to know which differences are real, and to show results per task as well as overall. Quinn is an expert Python programmer who knows the multiple-comparison problem well and wants the method documented well enough to cite on the leaderboard page.

8. Taylor teaches a graduate course on robot learning, where students often present results such as "15/20 vs. 12/20" as clear wins. Taylor wants to replay a recorded study in class, trial by trial, so that students watch the conclusion form and see when stopping early is safe. Taylor needs small example data that runs on a student laptop without large downloads, and would like to set up a simulated study with known true success rates so that students can check the tool against the right answer. Taylor is an expert in robot learning and statistics and builds lecture demos in Jupyter; the students know Python basics and introductory statistics.

9. Avery is a PhD student who studies how robot policies should be evaluated; Avery has no robot and relies on public data. Avery wants to replay existing trial logs, in their real order and in many random orders, and measure how early a decision could have been reached and how often it matches the full-data result. Avery needs a clean trial-level dataset in one documented format, and reports that state the method and settings behind each conclusion. Avery is an expert in statistics, fluent in Python, and reads the source code of the tools Avery uses.

## Suhang

1. Kenji is a machine learning engineer at an embodied AI startup. Every week he must decide whether a new checkpoint should replace the model on customer robots. He wants that decision backed by statistics, not by watching a few videos. He needs a clear ship-or-hold result with the error rate stated, plus a saved record of the trials behind it. Kenji is an expert Python developer who knows A/B testing.

2. Dr. Lee teaches an undergraduate robotics course and wants to show students why 20 trials are often not enough to claim that one robot is better. She needs a short demo on public data that shows the decision changing as trials come in. Dr. Lee is comfortable with Python and wants to run it live in class.

3. Ben is the systems administrator for a lab whose robot computers have no internet access. He wants to install evallog from a downloaded package. He needs few dependencies, a standard pip install, and support for Linux and macOS. Ben is highly technical but knows nothing about robot learning.

4. Ivan is a robotics researcher who is color-blind and often reads reports with a screen magnifier. He wants to read evallog's report without relying on color to tell two policies apart. He needs charts with direct labels and tables with clear text that stay readable when enlarged. Ivan is highly technical and comfortable reading statistics tables.

5. Victor is a user to design against: an eager student who wants his policy to look good. He checks the result after every trial, wants to stop the moment he is ahead, and might delete a bad session. The tool must keep its error rate valid even when results are checked after every trial, and it must record any deletion or restart made through the tool. Victor is technically capable, which makes this misuse easy.

6. Gina is a user to design against: a graduate student who wants to show that her policy beats a rival's. She is tempted to count trials from easy tasks for her policy and trials from hard tasks for the rival. She might also log trials for only one of the two policies. The tool must warn when trials are unpaired or the two policies were not tested under matching conditions. Gina has intermediate technical skills and knows how the experiments are set up.

7. Hannah is a researcher who scores trials with partial credit: 1 for full success, 0.5 for grasping the object but dropping it, and 0 for failure. She wants evallog to accept these scores instead of forcing her to round them to pass or fail. The statistics must stay valid for scores between 0 and 1. Hannah is highly technical and reads statistics papers when she has to.

8. Tom is a product manager at a robotics company. He signs off on releases but does not read code or statistics. He wants to decide whether to approve a release. He needs a one-sentence conclusion, such as "the new version is better, tested at a 5% error rate," and one simple chart. Tom's technical skill is low, but he understands the product and its customers well.

9. Lina is a first-year master's student who just joined a robotics lab. She wants to log her first evaluation without breaking anything. She needs a quick-start guide and examples she can copy. She also needs error messages that say what went wrong, such as a misspelled policy name. Lina has used Python in class notebooks but rarely uses the command line.

10. Diego is an undergraduate research assistant who resets the scene and records outcomes during long evaluation sessions. He wants to enter each result on a laptop next to the robot in a few seconds. He sometimes types the wrong outcome. He needs a safe way to correct the last entry, with the correction still recorded in the history. Diego is comfortable with computers but is not a programmer.
